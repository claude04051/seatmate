# Writing the episode

## The format constrains the writing

In animated mode the video is built from **10-second blocks, one narration line each**, and the
workflow requires each line to land between **8.6 and 10.0 seconds of detected speech**. A line
that runs long gets rewritten and regenerated, so writing to the budget the first time saves a
whole cycle.

At normal narration pace that's **roughly 22–26 words per line**. Practical consequences:

| Video length | Blocks | Narration lines |
|---|---|---|
| 60s | 6 | 6 |
| 90s | 9 | 9 |
| 2:00 | 12 | 12 |
| 3:00 | 18 | 18 |

**A 90-second episode is nine sentences.** That is the entire script. It's less room than
instinct expects, and it's why scope discipline matters more than anything else — you cannot
"briefly mention" anything at this budget. Nine sentences buys one mechanism explained properly,
or three explained badly.

Write each line as a complete spoken sentence that can stand alone, because it will be heard
over its own scene. Avoid sentences that split across blocks — the visual cuts underneath and
the thought breaks with it.

## Structure

The shape that works for science explainers, mapped onto blocks:

**1. The hook (block 1).** A tension, not a topic. The single most common failure is announcing
the subject: *"Black holes are one of the most fascinating objects in the universe."* That's a
table of contents, and viewers leave during it.

A hook poses something the viewer now needs resolved:
- *"There's a place where, if you watched someone fall in, you'd see them freeze — forever."*
- *"Your GPS would be wrong by 10 kilometers a day if we ignored one thing."*
- *"We've never seen a single atom. We've photographed a black hole."*

Never open with a greeting, a channel name, or "in this video we'll". Faceless channels have no
relationship to trade on in second one — only curiosity.

**2. The stakes (block 2).** Why this is strange, or why it should bother them. Convert the hook
into a question they'd want answered.

**3. The mechanism (the middle, most of the runtime).** The actual explanation, built one step at
a time, each step depending only on what's already established. This is where the episode either
teaches or doesn't.

Check every step: *would someone who didn't already know this follow it?* Assumed knowledge is
invisible to the person who has it — the physics equivalent of an undefined variable.

**4. The turn (~60–75%).** One counterintuitive consequence — the "wait, what?" beat. This is
what gets rewatched and quoted in comments. An episode without one is correct and forgettable.

**5. The reframe (last block).** Not a summary. Recasting what they now know so it lands somewhere
bigger, or opening the next question. Summaries tell viewers it's over and they leave before the
end screen; a reframe makes them sit through it.

Compare: *"So that's how time dilation works."* against *"Every second you've ever measured was
measured from somewhere. There is no clock outside."*

## Concrete beats abstract, always

Big numbers are meaningless as numbers. **10³⁰ kg** communicates nothing. The comparison does the
work:

- Not "the Sun is 1.4 million km across" → "if the Sun were a beach ball, Earth would be a
  peppercorn seven meters away"
- Not "neutron stars are extremely dense" → "a sugar cube of it weighs as much as Everest"
- Not "light is fast" → "light takes 1.3 seconds to reach the Moon, and 4 years to reach the
  next star"

Every abstract quantity gets an anchor in something the viewer has held or walked past. This is
also what makes a line quotable, which is what makes it shareable.

## Voice

Casual second person, deadpan, confident. Talking *to* one person, not addressing an audience.

- Short sentences. The format enforces this anyway.
- Say "you" — *"you'd see them freeze"* beats *"an observer would perceive"*.
- Contractions. Written-register narration sounds like a textbook read aloud.
- No hedging stacks: "it's sort of possible that maybe" — say it, or cut it.
- Don't perform excitement. "Mind-blowing!!" tells the viewer to be amazed instead of amazing
  them. Let the fact do it; deadpan delivery of something staggering is the register that works.
- Humor is fine and helps retention, but never at the cost of the explanation, and never a joke
  that requires knowing the answer already.

## Honesty

A science channel's asset is trust, and it compounds or collapses.

- Flag analogies as analogies. "This picture cheats, but it's useful" costs two seconds and buys
  the audience that would otherwise be correcting you.
- Say when something is unsettled. "We don't know" is a legitimate and interesting ending; a
  confident wrong answer is the one thing that can't be walked back.
- Distinguish established from speculative. Wormholes and multiverses are fine subjects, framed
  honestly as what they are.
- Don't overclaim significance. Not every result changes everything.

## Choosing topics

Best topics have a **counterintuitive answer to a question the viewer already half-asked.**
Familiar entry, surprising middle. "Why is the sky blue" is well-worn but the mechanism still
surprises people; "an introduction to quantum field theory" has no question in it at all.

Strong shapes:
- *Why does X happen?* — where the obvious answer is wrong
- *What would happen if X?* — a well-posed hypothetical with real physics
- *How do we know X?* — the measurement story, usually more interesting than the fact
- *Why can't we X?* — a genuine limit, not an engineering gap

Weak: anything phrased as a topic rather than a question, and anything whose honest answer is
"it's complicated" with no payoff.

For a channel, prefer topics that **chain**. An episode that ends on a real open question the
next one answers turns a viewer into a subscriber, which single strong videos rarely do.
